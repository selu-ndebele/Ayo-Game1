How this version plays

Traditional Ayo has regional variations, so here's exactly what this build does:

  • Sowing: pick any non-empty pit on your own row. Its seeds drop one-by-one into each following pit, moving counter-clockwise around all 12 holes.
  • Capturing: if your last seed lands in an opponent's pit and brings it to 2, 3, or 4 seeds, you capture it. If the pit just before it (still on the opponent's side) also sits at 2, 3, or 4, that's captured too: the chain continues backward as long as each pit qualifies.
  • Feeding rule: if your opponent's row is completely empty, you must make a move that sends at least one seed onto their side, if such a move exists.
  • End of game: when a player's row is empty and the opponent has no move to feed them, all remaining seeds on the board go to whoever still has seeds. Most captured seeds wins.
  • Champion: three match wins in a row makes a player Champion (ọba). A loss resets the streak.

The History of Ayo

Origins

Ayo, commonly called Ayoayo among the Yoruba, belongs to the wider African mancala family of sowing games. It became deeply rooted in Yoruba communities, especially in southwestern Nigeria.

The traditional board

A typical Ayo board has two rows of six pits. Players traditionally use seeds, stones or cowries, sowing the pieces around the board while planning captures and anticipating the opponent's next moves.

How people learned

The game was often learned socially: children watched older relatives and community players, then joined when they understood the patterns. Some boards were carved from wood, while informal games could be played in holes made in the ground.

More than entertainment

Ayo encouraged face-to-face interaction, patience, observation, arithmetic thinking and strategy. Evening games could attract family, neighbours and spectators who discussed moves and challenged skilled players.

Why preservation matters

As television, video games, smartphones, urban lifestyles and imported entertainment became more common, some traditional play declined. Much knowledge was passed orally, so local rule variations were not always formally documented.

Ayo in the digital age

Digital versions can help younger generations encounter the game again. This project keeps the strategic board game playable while also presenting its cultural story, so the technology becomes a tool for preservation rather than replacement.

Heritage note: Ayo has regional and community variations. This game documents one playable ruleset while recognising that living traditions may be taught differently by different communities.
